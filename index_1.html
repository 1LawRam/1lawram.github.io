A custom stress-testing rig — load cells, CircuitPython, and 3D-printed frames — built to capture real impact forces and turn crash damage into quantifiable structural data.

Drones have become more popular over the years, with new drone pilots who may crash during their initial learning period. Understanding how impact forces are distributed across a drone's frame can allow for further structural improvements that lessen damage and repair costs when a crash occurs. The frames were 3D printed as a cost-effective way of producing test articles for repeated crash testing.

Five load-cell sensors were placed in pre-determined locations within the frame — one on each arm and one in the center — and data was captured by dropping the instrumented frame from 5 feet. Results were analyzed with ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests; the Kruskal-Wallis test yielded a p-value of 0.001, indicating statistical significance. The center of the frame (Sensor 5) most frequently recorded the highest peak force, alongside physical cracks on two frames during testing — supporting the hypothesis that impact loading concentrates at the center.

An AI-assisted robotic arm that dispenses solder autonomously, in real time, by detecting the operator's soldering-iron placement gesture.

Built entirely from 3D-printed parts, custom PCBs, and computer vision running on an onboard depth camera — a third hand for the workbench that watches where the iron lands and feeds solder exactly there.

This project is currently in active development. Full documentation, results, and images will be released when completed.
